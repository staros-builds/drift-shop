POS costs are one of those expenses that creep up quietly. You sign up for $49/month, add a second register, enable a couple of add-ons — and suddenly you're paying $4,000+ a year before transaction fees. Here's how to bring that number down.

1. Calculate Your True Total Cost

Most shop owners underestimate what they pay. Add up, for the last 12 months:

  • Monthly/annual software fees
  • Per-transaction fees (percentage × your card revenue)
  • Per-transaction flat fees (15¢ or 30¢ × your transaction count)
  • Hardware purchases or leases
  • Add-on modules (loyalty, advanced reporting, etc.)
  • Per-register or per-location fees

This is your baseline. You can't reduce what you haven't measured.

2. Question the Transaction Percentage

The single biggest POS cost for most shops isn't the monthly fee — it's the percentage taken on every sale. A "free" POS charging 2.6% costs a $200,000/year shop over $5,000 annually.

Ask: is my software vendor adding value proportional to that cut? Or am I paying enterprise prices for a cash register app?

Systems that charge a flat fee instead of a transaction percentage (like Vendra at $350/year flat, no transaction fees from Vendra) flip this math entirely.

3. Bring Your Own Payment Processor

Some POS vendors require you to use their in-house processing at above-market rates. If your vendor allows it, connecting your own processor (like Stripe) at standard rates can save significantly — and it keeps you free to switch processors without switching software.

4. Audit Your Add-Ons

Pull up your last invoice and list every add-on module. For each one, ask:

  • Do we actually use this?
  • Is there a free alternative?
  • Is this feature included in a competitor's base price?

Common culprits: "advanced" reporting that's just a few extra charts, loyalty modules you set up once and forgot, and multi-location features for your single location.

5. Avoid Per-Register Fees

If you're paying $30–$60/month extra for each additional register, that's $360–$720/year per device. Systems that don't charge per register (Vendra included — $350/year covers your whole shop) eliminate this entirely.

6. Use Hardware You Already Own

Proprietary terminals can cost $300–$800 each, plus monthly lease fees. A POS that runs on a standard tablet or phone you already have — or a refurbished tablet for under $150 — keeps hardware costs near zero.

7. Watch Contract Terms

Multi-year contracts with early-termination fees are a cost even if they don't show up monthly — they prevent you from switching to something cheaper. Prefer month-to-month or annual terms with no lock-in.

8. Consolidate Your Tools

Many shops pay separately for: POS software, appointment booking, email marketing, a website builder, and inventory spreadsheets. An all-in-one platform that includes booking, online ordering, a storefront page, and inventory in one price replaces several subscriptions at once.

9. Re-evaluate Annually

Set a calendar reminder. Once a year, recalculate your total POS cost and compare it against alternatives. The market changes, your business changes, and loyalty to a vendor that's raising prices isn't a virtue.

Negotiate With Your Current Vendor

Before switching, it's worth asking your current vendor for a better deal — especially if you're out of contract:

  • Ask for the annual plan. Many vendors offer 10–20% off for annual billing, but don't advertise it prominently.
  • Ask them to waive add-on fees. If you're paying for modules you barely use, ask for them to be included or removed.
  • Mention you're evaluating alternatives. You don't need to bluff — just be honest that you're reviewing costs. Vendors often have retention offers.

If they won't budge, that's useful information too. It tells you how they value your business.

Don't Forget the Soft Costs

When comparing POS costs, factor in the less obvious expenses:

  • Training time. A complex system that takes days to learn costs you in labour hours — especially with turnover.
  • Downtime. A till that crashes during a rush costs real sales. Reliability has a dollar value.
  • Bookkeeping friction. Clean sales reports and tax summaries save your accountant's time (and your accountant's bill).

A system that's slightly more expensive on paper but dramatically easier to use can be cheaper in practice.
