Clothing stores have POS needs that sit somewhere between a coffee shop and a grocery store. You're dealing with sizes, colors, variants, seasonal inventory turnover, returns, and exchanges — plus the fitting-room-to-till journey that defines the boutique experience.

Here's what to look for in a POS for clothing stores, and how to avoid overpaying for features built for someone else.

Variants: The Make-or-Break Feature

A clothing POS lives or dies on variant handling. A single shirt might come in 4 sizes × 5 colors = 20 variants, each with its own stock level. Your POS must:

  • Track inventory per variant, not just per product. Knowing you have "12 shirts" is useless; you need to know you're out of medium blue.
  • Make variant selection fast at checkout. Scanning or tapping through size/color should take seconds.
  • Show variant availability clearly. When a customer asks "do you have this in large?", the answer should be one tap away.

If a POS system treats variants as an afterthought or a premium feature, it's not designed for apparel. Move on.

Returns and Exchanges

Clothing has one of the highest return rates in retail. Your POS needs to handle:

  • Easy returns linked to the original sale
  • Exchanges (different size, different color) without a convoluted process
  • Store credit and gift cards as return options

A clunky returns process doesn't just waste time — it turns a loyal customer into a frustrated one at the worst possible moment.

Seasonal Inventory

Fashion retail is cyclical. Twice a year (or more), you're clearing old stock and bringing in new lines. Your POS should make this painless:

  • Bulk product import so you're not entering 200 new SKUs by hand
  • Markdown and sale pricing that's easy to apply across categories
  • Clearance tracking so you can see what's moving and what's dead stock

Customer Profiles and Loyalty

Boutiques thrive on relationships. A POS that remembers customers helps:

  • Purchase history — "You bought the blue dress last spring; the new collection has a matching jacket."
  • Loyalty rewards that feel personal, not corporate
  • Gift cards, especially around the holidays

What Boutiques Don't Need

Skip the upsells:

  • Restaurant-specific features (table management, kitchen display) — obviously
  • Complex multi-warehouse logic — you have a stockroom, not a distribution center
  • Enterprise analytics suites — you need "what sold this week," not a data science department

The Real Cost

Clothing store POS pricing follows the usual pattern: $60–$150/month for software, plus 2.6%+ per transaction, plus extras for additional registers or "advanced inventory" (which for apparel should be *basic* inventory).

A boutique doing $250,000 a year can easily pay $6,000–$8,000 annually. That's a lot of margin walking out the door.

Staff Picks and Customer Service

The best clothing stores sell through service, not just product. Your POS can support that:

  • Customer notes. "Prefers sustainable fabrics, size M, allergic to wool" — notes that pop up at checkout turn a transaction into a relationship.
  • Wishlist and special orders. When you don't have the size, offer to order it. Track special orders in the system so nothing falls through the cracks.
  • Layaway. Still relevant for higher-ticket boutiques. If your POS supports deposits and partial payments cleanly, it's a real advantage.

Fitting Room Workflow

The fitting room is where clothing sales are won or lost. A POS that respects this workflow helps:

  • Quick lookup from the floor. Staff should be able to check stock from a phone or tablet without walking to the till.
  • Hold and reserve. "I'll hold this in the back while you keep looking" — the system should track held items so they don't get sold twice.

Online and In-Store Together

Even small boutiques benefit from a basic online presence:

  • A simple storefront page that shows your current inventory lets local customers browse before visiting.
  • Online ordering for pickup captures sales from customers who know what they want.
  • Consistent inventory across in-store and online — selling something online that's already sold in-store is a fast way to lose trust.
