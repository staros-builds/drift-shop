In a lot of neighborhoods, your customers don't all speak the same language. In a lot of cities, your staff might not either. Running a multilingual shop isn't just good manners — it's good business. Here's how to think about it practically.

Start with what matters most

You don't need to translate everything on day one. Prioritize:

1. Prices and product names — the thing customers look at most

2. Your return/exchange policy — misunderstandings here cause the most friction

3. Signage for key areas — entrance, till, restrooms, exits

4. Your online presence — website and ordering page, if you have them

Everything else can follow.

Your POS should speak your customers' language

If your point-of-sale system only works in one language, every transaction with a non-speaker becomes a pantomime. A POS that switches languages means staff can serve customers in the language they're comfortable with — fewer mistakes, faster checkout, happier everyone.

Hire for it when you can

A staff member who speaks the neighborhood's second language is worth more than any translated sign. If you're hiring anyway, language skills are a legitimate plus. And if a current team member is bilingual, make sure customers know — a small "se habla español" or "on parle français" on a name tag does real work.

Don't rely on machine translation for the important stuff

Auto-translate is fine for a rough product description. It's not fine for your refund policy, allergy information, or anything legal. For the handful of documents that really matter, get a human to check them — a bilingual friend, a community member, a freelancer for an hour. It's cheap insurance against embarrassing or costly mistakes.

Online: one shop, multiple doors

If you sell online, each language is essentially a separate storefront door leading to the same shop. Customers should be able to switch languages without losing their cart or getting dumped back to the homepage. Test the full flow — browse, add to cart, check out — in each language before you announce it.

It's an ongoing practice, not a project

Languages in a neighborhood shift. New communities arrive, second generations grow up bilingual. Revisit your language setup once a year: is the signage still right? Does the website match who's actually walking in? Small updates keep you current.
