Nobody opens a shop because they love doing payroll. But if you have staff, you need to know who worked when — accurately, without arguments, and without spending your Sunday evening reconstructing the week from memory.

Why paper timesheets fail

Paper timesheets rely on everyone remembering to write down their hours, legibly, honestly, on time. In practice: people forget, handwriting is ambiguous, and "I think I left around 5:30ish" becomes the basis for actual pay. It's not that staff are dishonest — memory is just unreliable.

What a time clock actually gives you

A simple clock-in/clock-out system:

  • Exact times — no rounding from memory, no "ish"
  • One source of truth — when there's a question about hours, you check the log together instead of debating recollections
  • Overtime visibility — you see it accumulating during the week, not as a surprise on payday
  • Accountability without surveillance — it's not about watching people, it's about having clean records everyone can trust

Make clocking in frictionless

The system only works if people actually use it. That means:

  • The clock-in point should be where staff already are — near the till, by the back door, on a tablet at the counter
  • It should take under five seconds — tap, done
  • Don't punish honest mistakes — if someone forgets to clock out, fix it together and move on. A system people fear is a system people avoid.

Handle the edge cases upfront

Decide your policies before they come up: What happens if someone forgets to clock in? How are breaks handled — paid or unpaid, and does the clock keep running? What about split shifts? Write it down, share it with the team, apply it consistently. Most time-tracking disputes aren't about the tool — they're about unclear rules.

Review weekly, not monthly

Glance at the hours every week. Catch the missed clock-out on Tuesday, not three weeks later when nobody remembers. A five-minute weekly review prevents the vast majority of payroll headaches.

Keep it separate from performance

Time tracking tells you when people worked, not how well. Don't use clock-in times as a proxy for judging staff — someone who clocks in at 8:58 instead of 9:00 isn't a problem. Keep the time log as what it is: an accurate record for fair pay.
