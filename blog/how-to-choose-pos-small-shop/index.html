Choosing a point-of-sale system is one of the few technology decisions that affects your business every single day. The till is where money changes hands — get it wrong and you pay in fees, friction, and frustrated staff for years.

This guide walks through choosing a POS system for a small shop, step by step, with no jargon and no sales pitch.

Step 1: List Your Actual Requirements

Before looking at any product, write down what your shop does on a normal day. Be specific:

  • How many sales per day? What's the average transaction size?
  • Do you sell services, appointments, or just products?
  • How many staff members need till access?
  • Do you need inventory tracking, or is your stock simple enough to eyeball?
  • Do customers order online or by phone?
  • Do you sell gift cards?
  • What languages do your staff and customers use?

This list is your requirements document. Every POS demo should be judged against it — not against a marketing feature list.

Step 2: Calculate Total Cost of Ownership

This is the step most buyers skip, and it's the most important one. For each candidate system, calculate:

Year 1 cost = setup fees + (monthly fee × 12) + (transaction percentage × your annual revenue) + hardware costs + add-on subscriptions

Year 3 cost = Year 1 cost × 3 (roughly — adjust for price increases, which are common)

The transaction percentage is usually the biggest number. A provider taking even a small cut of every sale will cost far more over three years than the subscription suggests. Always separate the payment processor's standard fee (unavoidable) from the POS provider's additional cut (avoidable — some providers charge $0).

Step 3: Test the Daily Workflow

Demos are choreographed. Trials are truth. During any free trial, run these real-world tests:

1. Ring up your five most common transactions, including discounts, returns, and split payments.

2. Add ten new products to inventory. How long does it take? Is bulk import available?

3. Hand the till to your least tech-savvy employee with no training. Time how long until their first successful sale.

4. Process a refund. It should take seconds, not a support ticket.

5. Run end-of-day closing. The report should reconcile without spreadsheet gymnastics.

6. Simulate your busiest hour. Is the interface fast under pressure? Are buttons big enough to hit accurately?

If a system needs a training manual for basic sales, it's the wrong system for a small shop.

Step 4: Check What's Included vs. Upsold

Many POS providers advertise a low base price, then charge extra for features small shops consider basic. Verify whether each of these is included or an add-on:

  • Inventory management and low-stock alerts
  • Appointment booking
  • Staff time-clock and scheduling
  • Customer directory
  • Gift cards
  • Online ordering
  • A public storefront or website page
  • Sales reports and analytics
  • Multiple languages
  • Automatic backups

Five "small" add-ons at $20/month each is $1,200/year in surprise costs. An all-in-one platform at a flat $350/year with everything included eliminates this entirely.

Step 5: Verify Data Ownership

Ask directly: "If I cancel, what happens to my data?" The acceptable answers:

  • You can download a complete backup at any time, including after cancellation.
  • Your sales history, customer list, and product catalog export in standard formats (CSV at minimum).
  • Nothing is deleted when a subscription lapses — at most, the system locks new sales while preserving everything.

If a provider can't answer clearly, or implies your data disappears when you stop paying, walk away. Your business records are not a retention tool.

Step 6: Evaluate Support Realistically

Small shops can't wait three days for a ticket response during a busy Saturday. Check:

  • Is there a real human available, and during your business hours?
  • Is there documentation written for beginners, not developers?
  • Does the system include built-in help and step-by-step guides?

Also consider the product's design philosophy: software designed to be beginner-proof needs less support by nature. If every term is explained in plain language and every screen is self-evident, most "support" questions never happen.

Step 7: Plan the Migration

Switching systems is less scary with a plan:

1. Export your product catalog and customer list from the old system.

2. Import into the new system and verify pricing, taxes, and categories.

3. Run both systems in parallel for 1-2 weeks.

4. Train staff during quiet hours — with an intuitive system, this takes under an hour.

5. Export and archive your full sales history from the old system before closing it.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • "Custom" pricing with no public numbers — it means the price depends on how much they think you'll pay.
  • Long-term contracts — month-to-month or annual with a clear exit is the honest structure.
  • Proprietary hardware requirements — locks you in and inflates costs.
  • Per-register or per-location fees — punishes you for growing.
  • No free trial — if they won't let you test it, ask why.
  • English-only interface — in 2026, multilingual support should be standard, not premium.

The Bottom Line

Choosing a POS system for a small shop comes down to three questions: What's my true three-year cost? Can my staff use it without training? Do I own my data? Answer those honestly for each candidate and the right choice usually becomes obvious. Flat pricing, beginner-proof design, and guaranteed data access are the markers of software built for independents.
