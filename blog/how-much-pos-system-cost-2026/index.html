"How much does a POS system cost?" sounds like a simple question. The honest answer: anywhere from $350/year to $10,000+/year for a small shop, depending almost entirely on pricing structure — not features. Two systems with identical capabilities can differ by an order of magnitude in total cost.

This guide breaks down every layer of POS pricing in 2026 so you can calculate your true cost before you commit.

The Five Layers of POS Pricing

Almost every POS quote is built from some combination of these five layers. Miss one and your budget is fiction.

Layer 1: Software Subscription

The headline number. Most providers charge monthly, tiered by features:

  • Entry tiers often lack inventory, appointments, or staff management — the features that make a POS useful.
  • Mid tiers add the essentials but may cap registers, users, or locations.
  • Top tiers unlock everything but are priced for much larger businesses.

Annual billing usually saves 10-20% versus monthly. But the subscription is rarely the biggest cost — it's just the most visible one.

Layer 2: Transaction Fees (The Big One)

This is where POS pricing gets expensive. Many providers take a percentage of every sale — on top of what your payment processor charges.

To be clear about the two different fees:

  • Payment processing fee: charged by Stripe, Lemon Squeezy, or your processor for moving money. Standard industry rates apply no matter which POS you use. Unavoidable.
  • POS provider fee: an additional cut some POS companies take on top of processing. Pure software margin. Entirely avoidable — some providers charge $0.

On $200,000 in annual revenue, even a small provider-side percentage translates to thousands of dollars per year — every year, scaling with your success. This single layer often exceeds all other costs combined.

Layer 3: Hardware

Options range from "use what you have" to proprietary terminal fleets:

  • Bring your own device: tablets and phones you already own. Cost: $0.
  • Provider hardware bundles: card readers, terminals, printers, cash drawers. Can run hundreds to thousands upfront, sometimes via lease.
  • Proprietary lock-in: hardware that only works with one provider's software. Switching providers means replacing everything.

For a small shop, bring-your-own-device is almost always the right answer. A modern tablet runs a full POS — the dedicated terminal is a choice, not a requirement.

Layer 4: Add-Ons and Modules

The base subscription often excludes features small shops consider basic. Common upsells:

  • Appointment booking
  • Loyalty programs
  • Gift cards
  • Advanced inventory
  • Staff management
  • Online ordering
  • Additional languages

Each add-on might be "$15-$30/month" — individually small, collectively $1,000+/year. Always price the system with every feature you'll actually use, not the advertised base tier.

Layer 5: Implementation and Extras

  • Setup and onboarding fees — some providers charge for what should be self-serve.
  • Data migration — importing your catalog and customer list.
  • Training — per-session fees for teaching staff.
  • Support tiers — priority support as a paid upgrade.
  • Price increases — the quiet layer. Many providers raise rates annually; check contract terms.

Three Realistic Cost Scenarios

Let's put it together for a hypothetical small shop doing $200,000/year in revenue, 2 registers, 4 staff.

Scenario A: Monthly + percentage model

  • Subscription across tiers and add-ons
  • Provider transaction cut on $200,000 in sales
  • Hardware (one-time, amortized)
  • Typical all-in range: $5,000-$8,000/year, growing with revenue

Scenario B: Monthly flat, no transaction cut

  • Higher subscription, but no percentage on sales
  • Add-ons as needed
  • Typical all-in range: $1,500-$3,500/year

Scenario C: Annual flat fee, everything included

  • One payment covers software, all features, all registers
  • No transaction cut from the provider
  • Bring-your-own-device hardware: $0

The gap between Scenario A and Scenario C over five years is routinely $25,000-$40,000. For a small shop, that's life-changing money — a renovation, a second location's deposit, or simply profit.

Why Prices Vary So Much

It's not about features. A $6,000/year system and a $350/year system often do the same core jobs: ring up sales, track inventory, manage staff, take appointments. The price difference reflects business models, not capability:

  • Venture-backed providers need growth and high margins to satisfy investors. Pricing reflects that.
  • Enterprise-focused providers build for large clients and price accordingly; small shops pay enterprise prices for a fraction of the product.
  • Lean independent providers with low overhead can price sustainably at a fraction — they need thousands of customers, not millions, to thrive.

You're not paying for better software at higher prices. You're paying for someone else's cost structure.

How to Get an Honest Quote

When evaluating any POS provider, demand answers to these questions in writing:

1. What is my total first-year cost, including every fee, on my actual revenue?

2. What is the transaction fee *you* take, separate from my processor's fee?

3. Which features I need are add-ons, and what do they cost?

4. Can I use my own hardware?

5. What are the price-increase terms?

6. If I cancel, do I keep my data? Can I download a full backup?

If a provider won't answer clearly, that tells you everything.
