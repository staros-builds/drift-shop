Inventory management sounds like something for warehouses and logistics companies. It's not. If you sell physical products, you do inventory management every time you wonder "do I have more of those in the back?" This guide covers the basics in plain language.

What inventory management actually is

At its core, it's just knowing three things:

1. What you have — every product, every variant, every size

2. How much of it — current stock counts

3. Where it stands — what's selling, what's sitting, what needs reordering

That's it. Everything else is detail.

Start with a clean product list

Before anything else, write down every product you sell. Give each one a clear name — "Blue T-Shirt M" beats "Tshirt blu med." If you sell variants (sizes, colors, flavors), list each variant separately. This list is the foundation everything else builds on.

Count what you have (yes, all of it)

Do a full physical count. It's tedious. Do it anyway — ideally when the shop is closed so nothing moves while you count. Write down the number for each product. This is your starting point, your "ground truth." Every system, no matter how fancy, is useless if it starts from wrong numbers.

Track every sale against stock

This is where a POS with linked inventory earns its keep: each sale automatically reduces the stock count. No separate spreadsheet, no end-of-day reconciliation. When the till and the stockroom talk to each other, your numbers stay current without extra work.

Set reorder points

For each product, decide: "when I get down to X units, I reorder." Your best sellers need higher reorder points (they move fast); slow movers can run lower. You don't need software to do this — a sticky note works. But having it recorded somewhere beats relying on memory.

Do cycle counts, not just annual counts

Instead of one giant stocktake per year, count a small section every week. Aisle one on Monday, aisle two on Tuesday. You catch discrepancies faster, and it never becomes an all-weekend ordeal.

Accept some shrinkage

Theft, damage, miscounts — a small percentage of loss is normal in retail. Track it honestly rather than pretending it doesn't happen. If shrinkage is growing, that's a signal to investigate, not to feel guilty.
