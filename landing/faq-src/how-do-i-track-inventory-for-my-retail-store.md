## Why Manual Inventory Tracking Fails

Most small shops start with one of these methods, and all of them break down:

- **Memory and gut feel** — works until you have more than ~50 products or more than one person selling
- **Paper counts** — outdated the moment the next sale happens
- **Spreadsheets** — better, but someone has to manually enter every sale, return, and delivery; one missed entry corrupts everything

The core problem: manual methods separate the sale from the stock count. By the time you update the spreadsheet, you've already made three more sales.

## What Good Inventory Tracking Looks Like

**Automatic deduction.** Every sale reduces stock instantly. No manual entry, no delay, no errors.

**Low-stock alerts.** The system warns you before you run out — not when a customer asks for something you don't have. Set a reorder threshold per product and get notified when stock dips below it.

**Product variants.** Clothing sizes, color options, and multi-pack quantities should track as separate stock items under one product listing.

**Receiving and adjustments.** When a delivery arrives, you log it once and stock goes up. Damaged or expired items get written off with a reason code, so your records stay accurate.

**Reports.** See what's selling fast, what's gathering dust, and what to reorder — without counting a single shelf.

## Setting It Up (The Practical Steps)

1. **Enter your products** — name, price, and starting stock count for each item
2. **Set reorder thresholds** — how low can stock go before you need more?
3. **Log deliveries** — add incoming stock through the receiving function
4. **Sell normally** — the POS handles the rest automatically
5. **Do a cycle count monthly** — spot-check a section of shelves against the system to catch shrinkage or errors

The initial product entry takes a few hours for a typical small shop. After that, the system maintains itself.

## Handling Special Inventory Cases

**Products with variants.** A t-shirt in 3 colors and 4 sizes is 12 stock items, not one. Your system should let you define variants under a single product listing, each with its own SKU and stock count. When the medium blue sells out, the system should show that — not just decrement a generic "t-shirt" count.

**Bundles and kits.** If you sell gift sets or multi-packs, the system should deduct the component items' stock when a bundle sells. Otherwise your component counts drift from reality.

**Consignment items.** Some shops sell goods on consignment. Your inventory system should tag these separately so consignment stock doesn't inflate your owned-inventory valuation.

**Seasonal products.** Holiday items, summer lines, back-to-school stock — these need clear labeling so you can run end-of-season reports and plan next year's orders. A good system lets you tag products by season and filter reports accordingly.

## Cycle Counting: The Maintenance Habit

Even with automatic tracking, do a monthly cycle count: pick one section of shelves, count it by hand, and compare against the system. Discrepancies reveal shrinkage (theft or damage), receiving errors, or items rung up under the wrong SKU. Catching these monthly keeps your data trustworthy. A full wall-to-wall count once or twice a year catches anything the cycle counts missed.

Shops that skip cycle counts eventually discover their system numbers and shelf reality have diverged — and then they're back to a painful full recount. Fifteen minutes a month prevents that.
