# Where Small Shops Actually Bleed Money (And How to Plug It)

Nobody needs to tell a small shop owner to watch costs — you're already doing it. But some leaks are so gradual you stop noticing them. Here are the quiet ones worth a second look.

## The subscription stack

Count every monthly subscription your shop pays: POS, accounting, scheduling, email, music licensing, cloud storage, the domain renewal you forgot about. Write them all down with amounts. Most shops find at least one they're paying for and not using, and two or three that overlap. A single all-in-one system can replace a surprising number of these — but even without switching, the audit itself usually saves money.

## Transaction fees: the invisible rent

If your payment setup takes a percentage of every sale, do the math on what that costs per year. Not per transaction — per year, in dollars. For a shop doing even modest volume, a 2-3% cut is one of the largest single expenses on the books, and it's the one owners think about least because it's deducted automatically. Systems with no transaction fee change this equation completely.

## Over-ordering slow movers

Every product sitting on your shelf is cash that isn't in your bank account. Look at what's been sitting for 90+ days. Mark it down and move it, even at cost — freed-up cash and shelf space beats dusty inventory every time. Then order less of it next time. Your reorder points should reflect reality, not optimism.

## The "we've always done it this way" processes

Manual end-of-day reconciliation. Paper appointment books that need transcribing. Re-entering online orders into the POS by hand. Each of these is staff time you're paying for that a connected system does automatically. Time your current process once — actually time it — and multiply by 365. The number is usually startling.

## Energy and the little things

Not glamorous, but real: lights left on overnight, heating/cooling an empty shop, a dripping tap, packaging you could buy in bulk. Walk through your shop after close once a quarter with fresh eyes and a notepad.

## What not to cut

Don't cut the things that make customers come back: product quality, staff who know their stuff, a clean shop. Saving $50/month by switching to worse bags that rip isn't savings — it's a tax on your reputation. Cut the invisible waste, protect the visible quality.
